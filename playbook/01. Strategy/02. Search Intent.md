# Search Intent

> Why someone is typing a query — and therefore what kind of page Google will rank for it.

## What it is

Search intent is the goal behind a query. The same phrase can mean very different things depending on intent: someone searching "mineral sunscreen" might want to learn how mineral filters work, find a specific brand's product page, compare formulas for darker skin tones, or buy SPF 50 right now. Each of those is a different intent, and each demands a different page type to rank.

The standard taxonomy has four buckets:

- **Informational** — the searcher wants to learn something. ("does mineral sunscreen leave white cast")
- **Navigational** — the searcher wants a specific website or brand. ("supergoop login")
- **Commercial** — the searcher is investigating before a purchase. ("best mineral sunscreen")
- **Transactional** — the searcher is ready to act. ("buy mineral sunscreen SPF 50")

Some practitioners add a fifth — **Local** — for queries with a "near me" or location implication.

## Why it matters

Intent dictates page type, and page type dictates whether you can rank. If you write a 2,000-word blog post for a query Google ranks product pages on, you will not rank — full stop. If you build a slim product page for a query Google ranks editorial roundups on, same outcome. Misreading intent is the most common reason a "well-optimised" page never gets traffic. Get intent right and the rest of on-page SEO becomes easier; get it wrong and no amount of optimisation rescues you.

For a D2C brand, intent also drives where the keyword sits in the funnel. Informational queries are top-of-funnel and earn trust; transactional queries are bottom-of-funnel and earn revenue. A healthy SEO strategy targets all four — but the *content* you build for each is completely different.

## Core concepts

- **The four-bucket taxonomy (informational / navigational / commercial / transactional)** — the working classification most teams use. Imperfect but operationally useful.
- **SERP signal** — the most reliable way to read intent is to look at what's actually ranking. If position 1-10 is product pages, the intent is transactional. If it's editorial roundups, it's commercial. Tools' intent labels are guesses; the SERP is the truth.
- **Mixed-intent queries** — some queries genuinely have multiple intents (Google ranks a mix of page types). These are harder to win and usually require the dominant page type plus strong on-page signals to compete.
- **Page-type mapping** — once intent is known, each keyword maps to a page type: product, collection/category, blog post, comparison, FAQ, tool, glossary, landing page. This mapping is the bridge from research to execution.
- **Intent shift over time** — Google's interpretation of intent for a query can shift. A query that ranked blog posts two years ago might rank product pages today. Re-evaluate intent on important keywords annually.

## A worked example

> **Scenario:** A D2C beauty brand has a list of 220 keywords from their keyword research. They need to know which page type to build for each.
>
> **Step 1.** They take "best mineral sunscreen" and Google it. The top 10 results are: Wirecutter roundup, NYT Wirecutter, Good Housekeeping, Allure comparison, a personal blog, a brand listicle, two beauty listicles, an editorial review, and a YouTube sunscreen review. **Verdict:** commercial intent, page type = blog comparison/roundup.
>
> **Step 2.** They Google "mineral sunscreen no white cast." The top 10 are: Saie product page, Supergoop product page, Amazon listings, beauty blog posts about white cast, two brand collection pages, two listicles about white-cast-free sunscreens. **Verdict:** mostly transactional with some commercial, page type = product or collection.
>
> **Step 3.** They Google "does mineral sunscreen leave white cast." Top 10: a Wirecutter article, a Reddit thread, two skincare blog posts, a brand FAQ, a YouTube explainer. **Verdict:** informational, page type = blog post / FAQ.
>
> **Step 4.** They tag each of the 220 keywords with intent and page type. The list now splits cleanly into ~90 product/collection candidates, ~75 blog-post candidates, ~35 FAQ candidates, and ~20 deferrals (low priority or unclear intent).

## How to do it

1. **Start from a research list.** You need a list of keywords already discovered (output of [Keyword Research](01.%20Keyword%20Research.md)). Intent classification happens *after* discovery.
2. **For each keyword, look at the SERP.** Tools' intent labels are useful as a first pass but they're often wrong on borderline cases. The SERP is ground truth. Run the query (in incognito, in your target country) and read what's actually ranking.
3. **Classify by dominant page type.** Look at positions 1-5. What kind of page wins? Product pages → transactional. Editorial roundups / comparisons → commercial. Definitions / how-tos / explainers → informational. Brand homepages or login → navigational.
4. **Note the SERP features.** Featured snippet, People Also Ask, image pack, video pack, shopping carousel, local pack — these are signals about intent and about where you'll need to compete. A query with a shopping carousel rewards product pages with strong product-feed data.
5. **Map to page type.** Convert intent into the page type your site will need to build (product, collection, blog post, comparison, FAQ, glossary, tool, landing page). This mapping is the bridge to clustering and content briefing.
6. **Flag mixed-intent queries.** If positions 1-10 are split between page types, mark the keyword as mixed-intent and downgrade its priority unless you have authority to compete in both formats.
7. **Save the artifact.** Add intent and page-type columns to your keyword spreadsheet. This becomes the input to clustering and content planning.

## Common pitfalls

- **Trusting tools' intent labels.** They're directional but get borderline cases wrong about a third of the time. Always validate the high-priority keywords by checking the SERP yourself.
- **Forcing one page type.** Founders often want every keyword to map to a product page because product pages convert. Google doesn't care what you want. If the SERP says "blog post," you write a blog post.
- **Ignoring mixed intent.** Trying to rank a single page for both informational and transactional intent usually means you rank for neither. Better to build two pages and link them.
- **Classifying without market context.** SERPs vary by country and language. Always check the SERP in the target geography, not your home country.
- **Doing it once and forgetting.** Intent shifts. Re-evaluate the top 20-30 keywords annually, especially after major Google updates.
