# AI Crawler Management

> Which AI bots can access your content, what they're allowed to do with it, and how you describe your site to them. The technical-infrastructure layer of the AI-search shift — distinct from how you write content for AI.

## What it is

AI crawler management covers the technical decisions and artifacts that govern how AI systems interact with your site. Three things, all current and evolving fast:

1. **AI bot access controls** — every major AI company runs its own crawler with a unique user-agent. `GPTBot` (OpenAI training), `ChatGPT-User` (OpenAI live retrieval), `ClaudeBot` and `Claude-Web` (Anthropic), `Google-Extended` (controls Gemini training, separate from Googlebot), `GoogleOther` (a non-search Google bot), `PerplexityBot` (Perplexity), `CCBot` (Common Crawl, fed into many models), `Bytespider` (TikTok/ByteDance), `Applebot-Extended` (Apple AI training, separate from Applebot), and a growing list. Each gets allow/disallow decisions in your `robots.txt` — and most teams haven't made the decisions deliberately.

2. **`llms.txt` standard** — a proposed file at `/llms.txt` (paralleling `robots.txt` and `sitemap.xml`) that summarises your site's content for LLMs in a structured Markdown format. Adoption is uneven but growing — Anthropic, Mintlify, and a number of dev-doc sites publish it. Some sites also publish a longer `/llms-full.txt` with deeper structured content. Not yet a formal standard, but the pattern is gaining traction.

3. **Page-level AI directives** — meta tags and HTTP headers that govern AI access at the URL level. `<meta name="robots" content="noai">` and `noimageai` (publisher-led; not universally honoured). `X-Robots-Tag: noai` HTTP headers (same intent, different delivery). Useful when the policy varies by page rather than site-wide.

The goal of this leaf isn't to recommend a single posture (allow vs block AI) — it's to make the decisions deliberate, and to ensure your declared policy actually matches what's in your `robots.txt`, `llms.txt`, and headers.

## Why it matters

Three reasons. First, **AI bot decisions affect what shows up in AI Overviews, ChatGPT search, Claude search, Perplexity, and Gemini**. Block `Google-Extended` and you opt out of Gemini training but stay eligible for Google search. Block `GPTBot` and ChatGPT can't train on your content but `ChatGPT-User` (live retrieval) is still allowed unless you also block that. Each combination has different visibility consequences.

Second, **most sites have inconsistent or accidentally-permissive policies**. Default robots.txt files don't address AI bots at all, which means full allow. Some platforms (Substack, Medium, certain Shopify themes) ship with partial AI blocks that don't cover the latest bots. The result: founders think they're blocking AI training when they aren't, or vice versa.

Third, **`llms.txt` is the cheapest way to give AI systems a clean view of your site** if you've decided you want them to understand and cite you. It doesn't replace robots.txt or sitemaps — it complements them with a human-curated description of the site's structure and key resources.

For Field & Sun, the practical questions are: does the brand want to be trainable by GPT/Claude/Gemini for visibility in AI Overviews and conversational AI? Is there a content-rights concern with training? And does the brand publish enough informational content to benefit from `llms.txt` curation? These decisions belong to the founder; the technical job is to make them visible and enforceable.

## Core concepts

- **Two flavours of AI bots: training vs retrieval.**
  - **Training bots** (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, Google-Extended, Applebot-Extended, CCBot) crawl content for use in model training. Blocking them prevents your content from being baked into the model.
  - **Retrieval bots** (ChatGPT-User, Claude-Web, PerplexityBot, AppleBot for Siri) crawl in real-time when a user asks a question. Blocking them prevents your content from being cited live in AI answers.
  - These are independent decisions. You can allow retrieval (be cited live) while blocking training (not be baked into the model).
- **`robots.txt` user-agents for AI.** Each bot has a documented user-agent. Examples (always verify current docs):
  - OpenAI: `GPTBot` (training), `ChatGPT-User` (live), `OAI-SearchBot` (live search index)
  - Anthropic: `ClaudeBot` (training), `Claude-Web` / `claude-web` (live retrieval), `Claude-SearchBot`
  - Google: `Google-Extended` (Gemini training opt-out — note: this is a token, not a separate bot; you control Gemini access by adding it to robots.txt rules), `GoogleOther` (a non-search Google bot), `Googlebot` (regular search — don't block)
  - Perplexity: `PerplexityBot`
  - Apple: `Applebot` (Siri / Spotlight, traditional), `Applebot-Extended` (Apple AI training opt-out)
  - Common Crawl: `CCBot` (feeds many models indirectly)
  - Bytespider, MJ12bot, Amazonbot, Meta-ExternalAgent, etc.
- **`llms.txt` format.** Markdown file at site root. Conventional structure:

  ```
  # [Site name]
  > Short site description.
  ## Section 1 (e.g. Documentation)
  - [Page title](https://...): one-line description.
  ## Section 2 (e.g. Optional)
  - [Page title](https://...): one-line description.
  ```

  An optional `/llms-full.txt` can include the full Markdown of key pages inline. Both files live at the site root, alongside `robots.txt` and `sitemap.xml`.
- **`noai` / `noimageai` meta and headers.** Page-level signals. `<meta name="robots" content="noai, noimageai">` declares no AI training and no AI image use. Major model providers' adoption is partial; major search engines mostly don't honour these directly, but publisher coalitions (e.g. some news organisations) use them as part of broader content-rights frameworks.
- **TDM Reservation Protocol (EU).** A different mechanism — `tdm-reservation` HTTP headers under EU copyright law (Article 4 of the DSM Directive). EU-specific; relevant for EU-based or EU-traffic-significant sites.
- **Content rights vs visibility trade-off.** The strict-block posture protects content but loses you visibility in AI search. The full-allow posture maximises citation but means your content is in training data forever. Most brands land in the middle: allow live retrieval, block training. Field & Sun's posture for v1 of the playbook is example-only — the founder makes the call.

## A worked example

> **Scenario:** Field & Sun audits its current AI crawler posture. The Shopify Sense theme ships with a robots.txt that blocks `/cart`, `/checkout`, `/admin`, `/search`, but says nothing about AI bots. The team hasn't decided their posture.
>
> **Step 1.** Decision conversation. The founder wants Field & Sun to be cited in AI answers (Perplexity, ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, AI Overviews) since the brand's content-led strategy depends on visibility. But she's wary of full training-data inclusion — once content is in a model, it's permanent and uncompensated. Verdict: allow retrieval bots, block training bots.
>
> **Step 2.** Robots.txt update. Add explicit rules:
>
> ```
> User-agent: GPTBot
> Disallow: /
>
> User-agent: ClaudeBot
> Disallow: /
>
> User-agent: Google-Extended
> Disallow: /
>
> User-agent: Applebot-Extended
> Disallow: /
>
> User-agent: CCBot
> Disallow: /
>
> User-agent: Bytespider
> Disallow: /
> ```
>
> Retrieval bots (`ChatGPT-User`, `Claude-Web`, `PerplexityBot`, `OAI-SearchBot`) get no explicit rule — full allow by default.
>
> **Step 3.** `llms.txt` generation. The brand publishes 25 blog posts plus product pages. They want LLMs that retrieve to find the canonical description fast. Generate `/llms.txt` with:
>
> - Site description (from `business_context.md` identity and `seo_foundation.md` SEO scope)
> - "Hero products" section listing the 2 hero PDPs with one-line descriptions
> - "Skincare guides" section listing the top 8 informational blog posts
> - "Sun care guides" section listing the 5 sun-care-related blog posts
> - "About" link
>
> **Step 4.** Page-level meta. No special `noai` directives — site-wide robots.txt covers training. Optionally add `<meta name="robots" content="max-snippet:-1, max-image-preview:large, max-video-preview:-1">` to encourage rich snippets in AI Overviews.
>
> **Step 5.** Validate via the `audit-ai-crawler-access` skill. Confirm robots.txt rules apply, llms.txt is reachable and valid, no contradictions between site-wide and page-level signals.
>
> **Step 6.** Document the decision in `businesses/field-and-sun/CLAUDE.md` so the next theme update or migration doesn't accidentally regress the posture.

## How to do it

1. **Decide your posture before you write a line of config.** Three working postures:
   - **Open**: allow all AI bots. Maximises visibility, accepts training inclusion.
   - **Visibility-only**: allow retrieval bots, block training bots. Most common middle ground for brands.
   - **Strict-block**: block all AI bots. Protects content, loses AI visibility.
   The decision belongs to the founder / content owner. The technical job comes after.
2. **Audit your current `robots.txt`.** What does it currently say about AI bots? On most platforms: nothing — which means full allow. Confirm or refute.
3. **Update `robots.txt` to match the posture.** Explicit `User-agent` blocks per bot, with `Disallow: /` for blocks and no rule (or explicit `Allow: /`) for allows. Group all AI rules in one section for readability.
4. **Decide on `llms.txt`.** If posture is open or visibility-only, publishing `llms.txt` is a small, cheap signal that helps LLMs understand your site. If posture is strict-block, skip it (and consider not publishing it at all, since LLMs can read it without crawling).
5. **Generate `llms.txt`.** Use the `generate-llms-txt` skill. Curate, don't dump — list 10-30 representative URLs with descriptions, organised by section, not all 612 indexed URLs.
6. **Decide on page-level overrides.** Most brands don't need them. Specific cases: a few high-value pages where you want stricter rules than site-wide; legal / compliance pages where AI training is explicitly disallowed regardless of site posture.
7. **Validate.** Run `audit-ai-crawler-access`. Confirm robots.txt rules parse cleanly, llms.txt is reachable at root and valid, no contradictions.
8. **Monitor in logs (if accessible).** AI bot user-agents appear in server logs alongside Googlebot. Verify in actual traffic that the bots you blocked are honouring the rules. Most major AI bots respect robots.txt; some smaller / less-reputable bots don't.
9. **Document the posture decision.** Add to per-business `CLAUDE.md` so the choice persists across theme updates, migrations, and team changes.
10. **Re-audit quarterly.** New AI crawlers emerge. Existing user-agents change names. Major model providers update their bot identifiers. The list is not stable.

## Common pitfalls

- **Default robots.txt with no AI rules = full allow.** Most teams discover this only after content is already in training data. Make the decision deliberately.
- **Blocking `Googlebot` thinking you're blocking Gemini.** Different bots. `Googlebot` is regular search; `Google-Extended` is the Gemini training opt-out. Block the wrong one and you tank organic traffic.
- **Inconsistency between robots.txt, llms.txt, and meta directives.** If `robots.txt` blocks training but a page-level meta says "noai" and `llms.txt` is publicly readable, signals are mixed. Pick one stance and align all three.
- **Stuffing llms.txt with every URL.** It's a curated summary, not a sitemap. 10-30 representative URLs is the sweet spot.
- **Forgetting that `llms.txt` is human-curated.** It's not auto-generated like `sitemap.xml`. A stale `llms.txt` is misleading; either keep it fresh or don't publish.
- **Treating "blocked in robots.txt" as a guarantee.** Major reputable bots honour it. Smaller / scraper bots may not. Some bots use rotating user-agents to evade. Robots.txt is a request, not enforcement.
- **Publishing `llms-full.txt` with sensitive content.** It's publicly readable; don't include anything you wouldn't put in a public blog post.
- **One-and-done auditing.** New AI crawlers appear monthly. Quarterly re-audit at minimum.
- **Not aligning with content-rights stance.** A brand that publicly markets "we own our content" while having a robots.txt that allows all training bots is sending mixed signals. Audit your messaging too.
